## A2 · NEWS · Six grid operators, one deadline

**Hook** (0-3s)
> On Monday, six companies have to explain why your power bill should pay for a data center.

**Why it's good**
There is a clock on this and almost nobody outside energy law is watching it. On June 18 FERC issued Section 206 show-cause orders to all six US grid operators at once, and did something unusual: it put the burden of proof on them. Normally a regulator has to demonstrate a rule is unjust; here the operators have 60 days to demonstrate theirs is not. Sixty days from June 18 is August 17. The question they have to answer is who pays for the substations and transmission when an enormous new load shows up, and the honest answer for the last two years has been "everyone else's bill." These orders reach about two-thirds of all load served under FERC-jurisdictional rates.

**The beats**
- 0-3s — The deadline and what is being asked.
- 3-15s — Six orders, six dockets, one day, unanimous. This covers roughly 200 million people.
- 15-35s — What a show-cause order does: FERC did not ask them to consider a change, it made them defend the status quo.
- 35-60s — Watch what actually gets filed. A defence means your bill keeps absorbing it; a reform filing means data centers start paying their own interconnection costs.

**The material**
- June 18, 2026: FERC issued Section 206 show-cause orders to all six US RTOs/ISOs — PJM, MISO, SPP, CAISO, ISO-NE and NYISO (McGuireWoods, Day Pitney, FERC).
- Dockets: PJM EL26-67-000, SPP EL26-68-000, NYISO EL26-69-000, MISO EL26-70-000, CAISO EL26-71-000, ISO-NE EL26-72-000 (McGuireWoods).
- The orders were unanimous (Utility Dive, Duane Morris).
- Two deadlines, not one: a generation-adequacy informational report due within 30 days, which fell on July 20, 2026; and the tariff justification or reform filing due within 60 days, which falls August 17, 2026 (Day Pitney, White & Case).
- Threshold precision — this matters, get it right on camera: in the show-cause orders FERC defined large loads as peak load above 50 MW interconnecting to transmission above 69 kV (Bracewell). The widely quoted 20 MW figure comes from the parallel rulemaking, Docket RM26-4, which is where FERC is taking comment on a generic rule (FERC RM26-4, Utility Dive). Saying "20 MW" while showing the show-cause orders is the error a comment will catch.
- Reach: the six regions cover roughly 200 million people across more than 30 states plus Washington DC, about two-thirds of the load served under FERC-jurisdictional rates (Duane Morris).
- Section 206 of the Federal Power Act is the provision that lets FERC find an existing rate unjust and unreasonable; a show-cause order under it flips the ordinary burden onto the incumbent to justify what it already does (McGuireWoods).
- Each operator must address: available spare generating capacity, how interconnection queues will be managed, how to keep new substation and transmission costs off residential bills, and how data centers will cover the full cost of grid work tied to their own connection (FERC).
- The last of those — full cost responsibility for the connecting load — is the point utilities and consumer advocates have fought over for two years, and FERC has now made it a federal question (FERC).
- Related context: the orders advance objectives set out in the Secretary of Energy's October 2025 Advance Notice of Proposed Rulemaking (McGuireWoods).
- Counter-argument to have ready: FERC framed this as speeding data centers up, not slowing them down — the stated goal is faster large-load interconnection, and the trade press headline is "accelerate," not "crack down" (FERC, American Action Forum). Anyone arguing this is FERC protecting ratepayers is only reading half the order. The honest framing is that it forces the cost question to be answered, in either direction.
- Second counter-argument: FERC deliberately did not impose a one-size-fits-all fix. Each region has different requirements and latitude to choose its path, so "six operators, one deadline" is true of the date but not of the substance (Bracewell).
- What to watch after Monday: whether operators file defences or reforms, and whether the generic RM26-4 rulemaking then supersedes whatever they file.
- Scale anchor for why 50 MW is nothing: a single large AI data center campus is planned in the hundreds of megawatts to over a gigawatt, so the threshold catches essentially every facility in the current build-out.

- FERC, Docket RM26-4 landing page (the 20 MW rulemaking) — https://www.ferc.gov/rm26-4

## A3 · NEWS · DeepSeek starts charging peak rates

**Hook** (0-3s)
> Starting Monday, the cheap AI costs more at busy times of day. Like electricity.

**Why it's good**
Everyone will cover this as "DeepSeek raised prices." The actual news is stranger and better: DeepSeek introduced time-of-use pricing. Peak hours cost double off-peak, on the stated reasoning of allocating resources "more reasonably" and pushing developers into quieter windows. That is not how software has ever been priced — it is how a utility prices electricity, and it appears the moment a token stops being a software product and becomes a claim on a fixed amount of hardware. The inversion is the hook and the pricing model is the payload: DeepSeek won the price war, could not buy its way out of the demand it created, and the US labs cut prices in the same window because they can still add capacity.

**The beats**
- 0-3s — Peak and off-peak pricing. For an AI.
- 3-15s — Takes effect Aug 16-17, up to 1,100% higher depending on model and time. OpenAI cut Luna by 80% two weeks ago.
- 15-35s — Why a utility model appears: fixed fleet, demand at roughly seven trillion tokens a week, and no way to buy more chips. Price becomes the only lever.
- 35-60s — The thing to carry: a token price is a compute-access price wearing a software costume. Watch whether the US cuts survive the memory and power squeeze.

**The material**
- August 6, 2026: DeepSeek warned developers of a significant API price increase without giving rates or a date (Dataconomy).
- August 13, 2026: the actual rates landed. Increases range from 50% to 1,100% depending on model, token type and time of day (Quartz, Reuters via Investing.com, Fortune).
- Effective date is reported as August 16 by Quartz and August 17 by other outlets — check DeepSeek's own notice before stating it. Say "early next week" if unsure.
- V4-Flash old pricing: $0.14 per million input tokens, $0.28 per million output (Quartz, BenchLM).
- V4-Flash new peak pricing: $0.44 per million cache-miss input, $1.32 per million output. Off-peak is half the peak rate (Quartz).
- V4-Pro old pricing: $0.435 per million input, $0.87 per million output. New peak cache-miss input: $1.32 per million (Quartz).
- DeepSeek's stated reason, quoted: it is adjusting pricing "to allocate resources more reasonably," with the tiered structure intended to shift workloads to less congested periods (Reuters).
- The peak/off-peak split is the genuinely novel part. Note that the cache-miss distinction is also doing work — cached input stays cheaper, so the pricing punishes exactly the traffic that is most expensive to serve.
- Demand context: DeepSeek matched an 80% OpenAI price cut within hours, then hit a surge reported at around 7 trillion tokens a week against a fleet reported at roughly 20,000 GPUs (XenoSpectrum). Both figures come from secondary reporting — attribute them or drop them.
- The other direction, same window: OpenAI cut GPT-5.6 Luna by 80% on July 30, three weeks after the 5.6 family launched, from $1/$6 to $0.20/$1.20 per million input/output tokens. Terra dropped 20%. The flagship Sol tier was unchanged (VentureBeat, Forbes, Unite.AI).
- Anthropic launched Claude Opus 5 on July 24 at $5/$25 per million input/output tokens (Unite.AI, note.com roundup).
- Flagship comparison for the "expensive ones got cheaper" line: GPT-5.6 Sol and Claude Opus 5 both sit at $5 per million input; Opus 5's output is $25 against Sol's $30 (Unite.AI).
- Counter-argument to have ready: this is not straightforwardly "China loses." DeepSeek raising prices while preparing for a possible IPO is also what a company does when it stops buying market share and starts demonstrating unit economics (edenai, explainx). A price rise driven by demand you cannot serve is a very different signal from a price rise driven by weak demand, and the honest read is that it is both a constraint and a choice.
- Second caveat: even after an 1,100% increase at the top end, V4-Flash peak input at $0.44 per million is still far below the US flagship tier at $5. "The cheap one got expensive" is a relative claim, not an absolute one — say so or the correction will arrive in the comments.
- Precedent worth naming: time-of-use pricing is standard in electricity and in cloud spot markets. The interesting claim is not that it is unprecedented, but that inference has now joined the category of things priced like a scarce physical resource.

## B3 · TUTORIAL · Memory your coding agent can't fake

**What you build**
Kage — persistent memory for a coding agent that lives in your repo as plain Markdown and gets checked against the actual code before it is ever served back.

**Hook** (0-3s)
> I renamed a function last week. Watch my agent confidently tell me the old name.

**Time to build** 20 minutes, one command

**The money shot**
Set a trap and spring it. Rename something, let the agent's memory file go stale, then ask about it — it answers confidently and wrongly. Install Kage, ask again, and the stale memory does not come back, because it was validated against the code and failed. Most memory tools cannot show you this demo because they cannot detect the staleness in the first place.

**The beats**
- 0-3s — The wrong answer, delivered confidently.
- 3-15s — Why: agent memory is usually a text file that nothing ever re-checks.
- 15-35s — One install command. It builds a code graph and wires itself into the agent.
- 35-60s — Same question, correct answer. Then the team part: it is committed to the repo, so it travels through git.

**Gotchas** Early-stage — 32 stars, though 766 commits and a claimed 340+ tests behind it, so it is real work rather than a weekend wrapper. GPL-3.0-only, which matters if you are putting it in a commercial repo; earlier releases were MIT and that change is worth mentioning. Kage Cloud is waitlisted, so do not build the video around it.

**The material**

## C1 · EXPLAINER · A data center is a heater

**The misconception**
That a data center uses electricity to do computing, and cooling is an unfortunate cost on top. It is closer to the reverse: essentially all the electricity becomes heat, and the computing is something that happens on the way.

**Hook** (0-3s)
> A data center turns almost one hundred percent of its electricity into heat. The thinking is a side effect.

**The analogy**
An electric space heater is 100% efficient by definition — every watt in becomes a watt of heat out. A data center is the same device with better manners. Nothing leaves the building as "computation"; a rounding error leaves as light down a fibre. The rest leaves as warm air, which is why cooling is not overhead sitting beside the power bill — it is the same watt showing up on the bill a second time.

**Where the analogy breaks**
It does not mean computing is wasteful. The physical floor for erasing one bit is real but sits roughly a billion times below what actual chips use, so thermodynamics is not the binding constraint — engineering is. And the heat is not always wasted: some sites now sell it into district heating, which is the one case where the space-heater framing becomes literally the business model.

**The beats**
- 0-3s — The claim, flat.
- 3-15s — Follow one watt in. Show where it can possibly go. There is nowhere else.
- 15-35s — So cooling is not a tax on the power bill, it is the second half of the same watt. That is why siting is about water and air, not land.
- 35-60s — Pairs to the news: this is what the grid operators are arguing about on Monday.

**The material**
- The core physical claim: almost all electrical energy consumed in a data center is released as heat, via resistance in circuits and other dissipative processes (IEEE, "Thermodynamics of information technology data centers").
- Consequence, stated in the same literature: because nearly all input energy becomes heat, cooling energy is a major fraction of total data center energy consumption.
- PUE — Power Usage Effectiveness — is total facility energy divided by IT equipment energy. A PUE of 1.0 would mean zero overhead.
- Efficient facilities today run PUE between 1.2 and 1.4 depending on location, cooling design and heat reuse (Score Group).
- State-of-the-art liquid-cooled deployments are quoted at PUE 1.03-1.08 for NVIDIA H100/Blackwell thermal management (industry analysis) — attribute this one, it is a best-case vendor-adjacent figure, not a fleet average.
- Liquid-cooled Blackwell racks operate at 120-150 kW per rack, with coolant exit temperatures of 55-65°C (industry analysis). For scale: a domestic electric shower is about 10 kW, so one rack is roughly a dozen showers running continuously.
- Those exit temperatures are directly injectable into 4th-generation district heating networks without industrial heat pumps — which is why heat reuse suddenly became practical rather than theoretical.
- Regulation making this concrete: newly built data centers in Germany face a waste-heat reuse obligation from July 1, 2026, though the required share is only 10-20%. Germany's Energy Reuse Factor mandate requires 10% ERF from July 2026 (velasolaris, techplustrends).
- Reported economics of heat reuse: on the order of €62,000+ per MW per year (techplustrends). Single-source figure — attribute or omit.
- Stockholm and Helsinki are the standing examples of data centers feeding municipal district heating networks (multiple).
- The physics floor for the "where it breaks" beat: Landauer's principle sets the minimum energy to erase one bit at kT·ln2, which is about 3 × 10⁻²¹ joules at room temperature. Real processors operate many orders of magnitude above this. State it as "roughly a billion times" rather than giving a false-precision multiplier.
- Counter-argument to have ready: "almost 100%" is doing real work in that sentence and a physicist will poke it. Small amounts of energy genuinely leave as light down fibre, as electromagnetic radiation, and as chemical change in batteries. The figure is a very good approximation, not an identity — say "essentially all," not "exactly all."
- Second counter-argument: someone will say PUE proves cooling is only 20-40% overhead, not "the same watt twice." They are right about the arithmetic and it does not damage the point — PUE measures the extra energy to move the heat, while the claim here is about where the IT energy itself ends up. Keep the two separate on camera.
- Connection to A2: this is why the FERC fight is about siting near water and generation rather than near users. The constraint is thermal, not computational.

## C2 · EXPLAINER · Why long chats get expensive

**The misconception**
That a model reads your message once, the way you read a message once. It re-reads the entire conversation from the beginning to produce every single word it says back.

**The analogy**
A dinner party where, before anyone can speak, every guest has to shake hands with every other guest. Ten guests is manageable. A hundred guests is not ten times worse, it is a hundred times worse — the handshakes grow with the square of the table. That is attention. Each new token consults every token before it, so the cost of a conversation grows not with its length but with the square of its length.

**Where the analogy breaks**
Real systems cheat, legitimately. The key-value cache means the model does not redo every handshake for every new word — earlier work is stored and reused, pulling per-token generation cost down to linear. Prompt caching goes further and is the one optimisation that gets cheaper the longer the context runs. So the honest version is: the quadratic term is real and it is why long context was hard, but the industry has spent years building machinery specifically to avoid paying it.

**The beats**
- 0-3s — The reframe: it re-reads everything, every time.
- 3-15s — The handshake table. Draw it growing.
- 15-35s — Why you feel it: the long thread that got slow, the bill that grew faster than the transcript.
- 35-60s — Then the honest correction — caching. And why it matters this week, with compute scarcity showing up directly in prices.

**The material**
- Attention cost in the prefill stage scales quadratically with input length (FastKV, arXiv 2502.01068, and the broader long-context literature).
- Concrete scale figure: processing 1 million tokens requires roughly 250x the attention computation of a 4,000-token context (Introl long-context analysis). Note this is 250x, not 62,500x — the quadratic term is on attention specifically, not on total model FLOPs, which is exactly the nuance most explainers get wrong.
- The KV cache stores key and value vectors from all previous tokens and reuses them, making generation incremental. Memory then grows linearly with sequence length and batch size (multiple).
- Reported KV cache memory cost: on the order of 0.3125 MB of VRAM per token in typical configurations (InventiveHQ). Attribute — it is highly configuration-dependent.
- The scale that makes it vivid: past roughly 128K tokens on a 70B model, the KV cache becomes larger than the model weights themselves (DigitalOcean).
- Prompt caching pricing, Anthropic: cache reads billed at 0.10x the base input rate, a 90% discount. Cache writes are billed at 1.25x base for a 5-minute TTL and 2.0x for a 1-hour TTL (Tokonomics, OpenRouter docs summary).
- Worked example to use on screen: Claude Sonnet 4.6 input at $3.00 per million drops to $0.30 per million on a cache read.
- Cache entries require an explicit `cache_control: ephemeral` marker and a minimum of about 1,024 tokens for most models — caching is opt-in, not automatic, which is why many people never see the discount.
- OpenAI's cached input discount has historically been 50%, though newest flagships are now also billing cached reads at 10% of standard input (multiple, and the figure differs by model — check the live pricing page before quoting).
- The quotable line for the caching beat: prefix caching "is the only optimization on this list that gets cheaper the longer the context" (industry guide).
- Counter-argument to have ready, and it is the strong one: modern serving means users rarely pay the raw quadratic cost. With KV caching, generating each new token is linear in context length, and the quadratic appears in total prefill work, not in the per-token experience. If the video says "your chat costs grow quadratically" without qualification, someone who serves models for a living will correct it, and they will be right.
- Second counter-argument: linear-attention and state-space architectures exist specifically to remove the quadratic term. The claim is about the dominant transformer architecture, not about all sequence models.
- Third: on a hosted API, the user-visible cost driver is usually that the whole conversation is re-sent and re-billed as input each turn — which is linear per turn and quadratic across the conversation. That is the version most viewers actually experience, and it is worth being the one you lead with, because it is both true and felt.
- Connection to A3: this is the machinery underneath DeepSeek's cache-miss pricing. It charges more for exactly the traffic that cannot reuse a cache.

## C3 · EXPLAINER · The digit that catches fraud

**The misconception**
That made-up numbers look random, and that looking random is what makes them convincing. It is the opposite — real-world numbers are lopsided in a very specific way, and fabricated ones are usually too evenly spread.

**The analogy**
Watch a number grow by percentages rather than by addition. To climb from 1,000 to 2,000 it has to gain 100%, so it spends a long time starting with a 1. To get from 9,000 to 10,000 it needs only 11%, so it barely pauses on 9. Anything that grows this way — invoices, populations, river lengths, expenses — piles up on the low digits. First digit 1 appears about 30.1% of the time; 9 appears about 4.6%. A fraudster inventing invoices reaches for 800 and 900 because they want the money, and flattens the curve.

**Where the analogy breaks**
It is a red flag, not a verdict, and it fails cleanly in predictable places: numbers with hard floors and ceilings, assigned numbers like ZIP codes, and prices deliberately set at $9.99. It also cannot catch a single theft — it needs a population of numbers to test. US courts do accept it, but as one tool among several, never as proof on its own. The honest ending is that Benford's law tells you where to look, and a human still has to go look.

**The beats**
- 0-3s — Fake numbers do not look random enough. That is how they get caught.
- 3-15s — The 30.1% / 4.6% split. Show the curve.
- 15-35s — Why it happens: percentage growth spends longer in the low digits. The 1,000-to-2,000 versus 9,000-to-10,000 image.
- 35-60s — Where it fails, and the live example — a bank was told this month that invoices behind its financing were not genuine.

**The material**
- The full first-digit distribution, for the on-screen chart: 1 at 30.1%, 2 at 17.6%, 3 at 12.5%, 4 at 9.7%, 5 at 7.9%, 6 at 6.7%, 7 at 5.8%, 8 at 5.1%, 9 at 4.6%.
- The mechanism in one line: the law holds for data spanning several orders of magnitude that grows multiplicatively — the distribution is uniform in the logarithm, not in the number.
- The classic fraud framing, near-verbatim from the ACFE: a fraudster submitting fake invoices will not submit them for $100 or $200, he will "go big" and submit for $800 or $900, and doing that enough times upsets the natural order of the digits.
- Named after Frank Benford, who published in 1938, though Simon Newcomb observed the pattern in 1881 — the wrong-name detail is a nice fifteen-second aside if you need one.
- Scale of the problem it is used on: the ACFE's Occupational Fraud 2026 report analysed 2,402 real cases across 143 countries with documented losses over $3.4 billion (ACFE).
- Median loss per case in the 2026 report: $104,000, down from $145,000 in 2024 (ACFE).
- Billing schemes remain among the most significant fraud risks by combined frequency and financial damage (ACFE) — billing schemes are precisely the invoice-population case Benford's law is built for.
- Admissibility: Benford's law is accepted as evidence in US criminal cases at federal, state and local level, but a goodness-of-fit test alone is explicitly not conclusive and is one of several investigative tools (forensic accounting literature).
- Documented limitations to state on camera: it fails on data with built-in maximums and minimums; it cannot detect a one-off embezzlement or a handful of amended entries; and non-conformity has innocent explanations as often as guilty ones.
- The live hook, with the caveat attached: on August 4, 2026, Bloomberg reported that Jefferies was told some invoices underpinning its financing to iron ore trader Sapphire Minmetals were not genuine, after Jefferies checked them with Vitol Group.
- Sapphire Minmetals is ultimately owned by Rakesh Sethi through the Singapore company Sino Global Holdings Pte (Bloomberg).
- The denial, exact: "We categorically and strongly deny the above rumors," Sapphire Minmetals chairman Sethi said in an email, adding "This is untrue and unsubstantiated." Include this. It is an unproven allegation and saying so costs nothing.
- Context: the exposure sits in Jefferies' Point Bonita fund, which previously disclosed exposure to bankrupt auto-parts maker First Brands Group (Bloomberg).
- Important honesty point for the script: there is no public reporting that Benford's law was used to detect the Sapphire invoices. It was invoice verification with a counterparty. Use the story as an illustration of the category of fraud the law is designed for, not as a claim about how this case broke — that distinction is the difference between a good explainer and a false one.
- Counter-argument to have ready: Benford's law has been badly misused in public, particularly in election-fraud claims, where it does not apply well because vote counts per precinct are bounded and do not span enough orders of magnitude. Naming this pre-empts the most common bad-faith comment and demonstrates you understand the limits.

## D1 · LONGFORM · The rating nobody was meant to read

**Hook** (0-15s, the cold open before any title card)
> There is a credit rating agency with about twenty analysts that graded more than three thousand private credit investments in a single year. **The thesis**
Private letter ratings did not inflate because someone was deceiving investors — there are no investors reading them. They inflated because the only audience is a regulatory capital formula, and the company buying the rating is the same company the rating lets hold less capital against.

**Why it holds ten minutes**
It has a genuine turn in the middle. The first three minutes look exactly like a 2008 rerun — small agency, fees for grades, fired whistleblowers — and the viewer arrives already knowing this story. The second act takes it away from them: these ratings are unpublished, so the fraud framing does not fit, and the buyer of the rating is its beneficiary. The back half brings in material almost no coverage uses: the NAIC's own 2024 study, which sampled 109 private letter ratings and found 106 rated higher than its own analysts assessed, with some inflated by six notches — then the arithmetic that makes it worth doing, and then the fact that the NAIC pulled that report off its own website and never republished it. The third act follows the money offshore, and the last two minutes are live: the SEC denied Egan-Jones' application three days ago.

**The chapters**
- 0:00-0:45 — Twenty analysts, three thousand ratings, forty billion dollars, zero published pages. Sit on the arithmetic.
- 0:45-2:00 — What a private letter rating is and why it exists: insurers must hold capital against what they own, and how much depends on a letter grade. No grade, no cheap capital. Nobody outside the insurer and the regulator ever sees it.
