## A2 · NEWS · TSMC as the junior partner

**The script**
On August 11th, Sony and TSMC signed a binding agreement to build a chip plant together in Koshi City, in Kumamoto Prefecture in Japan, and the interesting thing is not the plant, it is the ownership. Sony holds 60 percent. TSMC holds 40 percent. TSMC is the largest contract chipmaker in the world, the company that fabricates the processors in your phone and very nearly every serious AI accelerator on the market, and in this venture it is the minority partner, on Sony's campus, in a company that Sony controls, consolidates as a subsidiary, and appoints the representative director of. The joint venture has a name, Advanced Vision Semiconductor Manufacturing Corporation, and a target of volume production in 2029.

What it will build is image sensors, which are the chips that turn light into an electrical signal inside a camera. Sony is the benchmark supplier of these to the phone industry, and the reason is a design approach called stacking, where the layer of pixels that catches the light is bonded to a separate logic layer underneath it using copper-to-copper connections, so the sensing and the processing sit on top of each other rather than side by side. Sony has historically done all of this itself, from research through to fabrication, in its own plants. On the money, it is worth being careful, because the headline figure that travelled was roughly one trillion yen, or about six and a half billion dollars, and the individually disclosed contributions are smaller than that: Sony is putting in around 465 billion yen, which includes transferring in the fab it recently built at Koshi City, and TSMC around 282 billion yen in cash, which comes to 747 billion. The release says further contributions come in phases based on market demand, and that additional investment is being discussed with Japanese government support, in an amount nobody has disclosed.

The detail that makes this worth explaining is that these two companies already have a joint venture in the same prefecture, with the control running the other way. It is called JASM, it was announced in November 2021, and Sony put in about 500 million dollars for roughly a 20 percent stake, which was diluted to 6.0 percent after Toyota and Denso came in and TSMC's own holding rose to about 86.5 percent. So within a few miles of each other, Sony and TSMC now run two ventures: one where TSMC owns nearly everything and Sony holds a sliver, and one where Sony owns the controlling stake and TSMC is the minority. Sony's group chief executive Hiroki Totoki explained the logic on the earnings call back on May 8th, when the memorandum was first signed, saying: "And last fiscal year, I talked about the fab-light strategy, which we want to pursue. And the JV with TSMC is the first step towards this fab-light strategy."

That phrase, fab-light, is where I would push back, and so would some analysts. Hideki Yasuda of Toyo Research Advice described the venture as a virtually risk-free investment that lowers Sony's burden of staying competitive in a business that demands enormous capital spending, while securing steady revenue for TSMC. Read it that way and this is less a strategic pivot than a way of getting somebody else to fund 40 percent of a factory. And there is a genuine tension in the label, because a company that owns 60 percent of a fab, controls it, and consolidates it onto its own balance sheet has not really gone light on fabs. It has found a partner for part of the cheque. TSMC's board approved this on the same day it approved roughly 29.4 billion dollars of capital expenditure for 2026, which is a useful reminder of the scale each side is operating at — for Sony this is a defining commitment, and for TSMC it is a line item.

What happens next is slower than the announcement makes it feel. The parties describe the venture's actual establishment as targeted within fiscal 2026 and still subject to customary closing conditions, and volume production is not expected until 2029. That is roughly three years before this plant ships a single sensor, into a market nobody can currently forecast — Sony's imaging and sensing division did well in the last fiscal year, with sales up 20 percent to about 2.15 trillion yen and record operating income of around 357 billion, up 37 percent, but that is a statement about phone demand in 2025 and 2026, not about 2029. The Japanese government subsidy is still described as being considered, with no figure attached.

I think the thing to take from this is narrower than fab-light and more interesting than a factory announcement. Sony is the rare company that kept making its own crown-jewel component instead of outsourcing it, and the position it is now moving to is not outsourcing either — it is co-owning capacity with the company that would otherwise be its supplier, while keeping control. That is a third option most people do not think about when they imagine the choice between building your own plant and buying from a foundry. Whether it turns out to be clever or just expensive depends almost entirely on what phone cameras are worth in 2029, and I would not trust anyone who tells you they know that.

## A3 · NEWS · Open weights with a revenue clause

**The script**
On August 12th, Alibaba released the weights for its biggest model, Qwen3.8-Max. Weights are the numbers a model is actually made of, so having them means you can download the thing, run it on your own machines, and build on it without asking anyone for permission. What came alongside them is the interesting part. It is not the Apache 2.0 licence Alibaba's flagship models have carried for the last two years, and the sentence doing the work in the new one reads: "If the licensee or affiliates conduct Model as a Service or AI Work Assistant business with aggregate revenue exceeding US$50,000,000 during any consecutive twelve months, they shall obtain a separate license from Qwen before Using the Software for commercial purpose." Put plainly: the weights are free to download, free to run, and free to build a business on, right up until that business clears fifty million dollars a year, and then you go and negotiate with Alibaba.

The before-picture matters, because this is a change rather than a starting position. Qwen2.5, from September 2024, and Qwen3, from April 2025, both shipped under plain Apache 2.0 — a standard permissive licence, which means do what you like, sell it if you can, just keep the copyright notice attached. No revenue conditions, no rules about what field you used it in. The change is also targeted rather than blanket. The smaller Qwen3.8-27B, published two days later on August 14th, is still ordinary Apache 2.0, and only the flagship carries the new terms. That flagship is a 2.4-trillion-parameter sparse mixture-of-experts model, which is a way of saying it is enormous but only switches on a fraction of itself for any given word — about 95 billion parameters at a time — and it can hold up to a million tokens in front of it at once, a couple of novels' worth of context. Alibaba previewed it in Shanghai on July 19th, started selling access on August 3rd, and put the weights out about nine days after that.

The clever part is in the definitions, because they decide who is actually caught. The licence says Model as a Service means "giving a third party access to language model inference or fine-tuning via API or hosted endpoint in a manner allowing meaningful control over inputs, parameters, or training data" — renting the model out to other people, in other words. An AI Work Assistant is "an independent AI-powered product primarily designed for AI-assisted coding or office productivity", with single-purpose tools, domain-specific assistants, and products where AI is merely a feature explicitly excluded. Read those two together and the licence is pointed at exactly two businesses: people reselling access to the model, and people building coding assistants and office copilots. Using it inside your own company is exempt, as long as that use does not make the software, its outputs, or its capabilities available to anyone outside. A second, lower threshold asks for credit rather than money: past 100 million monthly users or twenty million dollars of monthly revenue, the model's name has to be shown prominently in your product.

Now the thing I would want said before somebody says it in the comments, and it is two things. The first is that this is not new and Alibaba is not the strictest. Meta has run the same mechanism since Llama 2 in 2023 — cross 700 million monthly active users and you need a separate licence from Meta — and nobody calls Llama closed. Moonshot's Kimi K3 licence, published on July 28th this year, sets its bar at twenty million dollars, less than half of Alibaba's. The second is a correction to reporting I have seen repeated, which is that Alibaba is charging a revenue share of up to 30 percent. That number is not in the licence. It appears only in secondary coverage describing what such an agreement might include, and as far as I can establish the real terms for companies that cross the line are still unpublished and under negotiation. I would not repeat it as Alibaba's number, because it is not one.

Where this does bite is on the word open, and the Open Source Initiative, the group that maintains the definition of open source, has been consistent about this for years. Its executive director, Stefano Maffulli, said of the Llama 2 licence that if it were genuinely open source it "wouldn't have any restrictions on commercial use", pointing at points five and six of the Open Source Definition, which forbid a licence from discriminating against particular users or against particular fields of work. A clause that switches on at a revenue number fails that test the same way. Worth saying that the OSI has not published anything about this specific Qwen licence, so that is the principle applied rather than a ruling handed down. For context on why access matters at all, Alibaba's own price is two dollars per million input tokens and six per million output tokens — tokens being the chunks of text models are billed by, a bit under a word each — well under the American frontier models it benchmarks itself against.

I think the useful way to hold this is that open weights and open source were never the same thing, and the industry has spent three years letting the two words blur, because the blur was commercially convenient for everybody. What Alibaba has done is make the gap legible by writing a dollar figure into it. Fifty million dollars of model-serving revenue is a line almost nobody reaches, so in practice this changes nothing for researchers, for startups, or for anyone watching this. But it does tell you what these releases are for. They are distribution, priced at zero until you are big enough to be worth billing, and the licence is where the business model was hiding the whole time.

## B1 · TUTORIAL · Ask Claude what you saw

**The script**
Here is the job this does. It is four in the afternoon, and at some point this morning a terminal printed an error that you glanced at and closed, and you cannot remember what it said or which window it was in. Or somebody said a number out loud on a call and you did not write it down. What we are building is a recorder that runs locally and has been watching your screen the whole time, connected to Claude so you can search it by asking. You type "what did I see in the last five minutes" and you get an answer drawn from what was actually on your display. That sentence is not my example — it is the test prompt the project's own README suggests you run first.

The tool is called Screenpipe, and what it captures is more than screenshots. It records screen frames and audio, and it reads the accessibility tree, which is the structured description of what is on screen that assistive software uses, with optical character recognition as a fallback when that is not available. The distinction matters more than it sounds: reading the accessibility tree means the text is retrieved as text rather than guessed at from pixels, so searching it later actually works. Everything lands in a SQLite database on your own disk, with the screenshots written out as JPEG files. The reason to do it this way rather than the obvious alternative — screenshotting into a notes app, or paying for a meeting recorder — is that you end up with a queryable database rather than a folder of images, and Claude can talk to that database through an MCP server, which is the standard way tools expose themselves to an AI assistant. The tools it exposes include searching content, listing meetings and exporting video.

The build is short but there is one step where nearly everybody stalls: macOS permissions. You start the recorder, macOS prompts for screen recording access, and you grant that plus microphone and accessibility in System Settings under Privacy and Security. The failure mode is nasty because it is silent: there is an open issue on the project describing users who finish onboarding, see a healthy green status, and never actually got screen recording permission, so the thing sits there capturing nothing while looking fine. If a toggle will not take, the documentation gives you a real fix — you reset the permission from the command line with tccutil and relaunch, because macOS ties these grants to an application's bundle identifier, and switching between the release, beta and development builds means switching identifiers and re-granting everything. Before you go near Claude, check the recorder is genuinely running by asking its local API for your audio devices. If that comes back empty or refuses the connection, nothing downstream will work.

The moment that matters comes after you have let it record for ten or twenty minutes of ordinary work. You register the MCP server with Claude, start a fresh session, and ask what you saw in the last five minutes. A correct answer names actual things — the specific error text, the document you had open, the tab you switched to. A wrong answer is easy to spot because it is generic, some version of not having access to that, which means the tool call is not reaching a running recorder and you are back to checking the previous step. The other thing that shows well is exporting a video clip of a time range straight out of the history, because it turns an abstract database into something you can watch.

Once it is standing up, the same setup does a handful of other real jobs. It can find and summarise a specific past meeting by what was said in it rather than when it happened, using the meeting and speaker search tools. It can run small automations the project calls pipes, and its own example is a pipe that updates Linear every time you work on a particular task. And because it exposes the accessibility tree rather than just text, Claude can locate on-screen interface elements, not only read them.

The honest costs are worth stating, and there are three. The resource cost is modest: the project quotes five to ten percent CPU and half a gigabyte to three gigabytes of memory while recording, with disk running about 300 megabytes per eight hours in its event-driven mode, against roughly two gigabytes if you record continuously. Its own two sources disagree on the monthly total — the README says about 20 gigabytes a month, the documentation site says around 30 — so the honest answer is somewhere in that range. The licensing cost is the one that surprises people: despite living on GitHub, this is not open source. It is source-available, free only for personal non-commercial use, education or research, or a seven-day evaluation, and business use runs 25 dollars a month for standard, 50 per seat for pro, and 150 per seat for enterprise.

The third cost is the one I would actually think hardest about, and it is not money. This thing records your screen, your microphone and your keystrokes continuously, and stores all of it unencrypted on local disk by default, with encrypted sync as an opt-in extra. Anyone who gets access to that machine gets months of your working life, every password field you fumbled into the wrong box, every conversation the microphone happened to catch. Its headline claim is 100 percent local by default with nothing sent to external servers, and that is true of your recordings — but product analytics through PostHog and crash diagnostics through Sentry are on by default and have to be turned off in settings. There is also an open bug filed on August 13th 2026, against macOS 15.7.9 and Screenpipe 2.6.11, reporting that Chrome incognito windows kept being recorded even with the exclusion setting on. I think this is genuinely useful software, and I would run it deliberately for a working session and stop it afterwards, rather than leaving it on and forgetting it exists.

**How you set it up**
Prerequisites: a Mac, Node.js with npx available on your PATH, and Claude Code installed with the claude command working. No account or API key is needed for the local path.

1. Start the recorder with `npx screenpipe record`. macOS should immediately prompt for Screen Recording permission — if it exits silently without prompting, you have hit the known onboarding bug and should go straight to step 2 manually.
2. Grant three permissions in System Settings → Privacy & Security: Screen & System Audio Recording, then Microphone, then Accessibility. This is the step where most people get stuck.
3. If a permission toggle will not stick or the app is not listed, run `tccutil reset ScreenCapture <bundle-id>` — substituting `Microphone`, `Accessibility` or `ListenEvent` for the other grants — then relaunch. The bundle identifier is `screenpi.pe` for the release build, `screenpi.pe.beta` for beta, and `screenpi.pe.dev` for dev.
4. Confirm the recorder is actually running with `curl http://localhost:3030/audio/list`. A correct result lists your audio devices; connection refused or an empty response means it is not recording yet, and nothing after this will work.
5. Run `npx screenpipe setup`.
6. Register the MCP server with Claude Code: `claude mcp add screenpipe -- npx -y screenpipe-mcp@latest`. Note that the documentation site shows this command with an added `--transport stdio` flag and the README does not — start with the README version, and only add the flag if your Claude Code version rejects it.
7. Start a new Claude Code session so it picks up the newly registered server.
8. Let it record ten to twenty minutes of ordinary work, then ask Claude: `what did i see in the last 5 mins?` A correct answer names specific things that were actually on your screen. A generic "I don't have access to that" means the tool call is not reaching the recorder — go back to step 4.
9. If you want private browsing excluded, turn that setting on and then verify it actually works before trusting it, because there is an open bug reporting that it silently fails.

## B2 · TUTORIAL · Spreadsheets your agent actually builds

**The script**
Here is the job. You ask an AI agent for a financial model, and what you get back is a table in the chat window, or if you are lucky a CSV file with numbers baked into the cells. What you wanted was an actual Excel workbook where the total row contains a SUM formula, so that when you change an input the output moves. That gap — between a picture of a spreadsheet and a spreadsheet — is what this build closes. The tool is called OfficeCLI, and it writes real Word, Excel and PowerPoint files from the command line, with no copy of Microsoft Office installed anywhere on the machine and no cloud round-trip.

The thing to understand about it is that it is not an AI tool at all, which is exactly why it works well with one. There is no API key to configure and no model inside it. It is a deterministic local binary, built on .NET and compiled into a self-contained executable, that manipulates the underlying Office file format directly. The intelligence comes from whatever agent is driving it, and the agent drives it through an MCP server that ships in the box. The reason this beats the obvious alternative matters: the obvious alternative is letting the agent write Python against openpyxl or python-pptx, which works, but you get a different ad-hoc script every time, one library per file format, and — the real problem — the agent has no idea what the finished document actually looks like. It writes the file blind and hopes. OfficeCLI gives it a single consistent command surface across all three formats, plus a rendering engine that turns any document into HTML or a PNG, so the agent can look at what it just made.

The capability list is where I would be sceptical if I had not checked, because "generates Office files" usually means "writes values into cells." Here the README claims over 350 built-in Excel functions evaluated automatically on write, including dynamic arrays and the financial and statistical functions, so formulas are genuinely computed rather than stored as text. Pivot tables are built as native objects in the file format, with multi-field rows and columns, ten aggregation types, date grouping and calculated fields. Charts are real chart objects, including trendlines, error bars, waterfall and candlestick. And all three formats support reading and editing existing files, not just creating new ones, which is what makes it useful against documents somebody else sent you.

The setup takes about five minutes. You install the binary with a single shell command, check the version comes back, then register the MCP server with Claude Code using its own subcommand and confirm the registration. Then you restart your Claude session so it picks the server up. The one place to slow down is a locking behaviour that trips people up constantly: the CLI keeps a resident process holding the file open, so a sequence of edits can succeed while any other program reading that file from disk still sees the old contents, and a later edit can fail outright with a complaint that the file is held. The fix is explicit — you save or close the file through the tool before anything else touches it — and knowing that in advance turns a baffling ten minutes into a non-event.

The moment to watch for is a split screen. On one side, you ask Claude for something with real structure — quarterly revenue by region, a total row, a pivot by region, and a chart. On the other, the file appearing on disk and opening in whatever spreadsheet application you use. Then click into the total cell so the formula bar shows a SUM rather than a number. That is the entire payoff in one frame: not that the numbers are there, but that the spreadsheet is alive. If you want the moment without leaving the terminal, the tool has a watch mode that opens a live auto-refreshing preview of the file as it changes.

Beyond the demo, the same setup does a few genuinely useful things. It will merge data into a Word template by replacing placeholder tokens throughout a document in one pass. It will build native pivot tables straight from a data range. It will render any Office file to HTML or PNG so you can eyeball a document without opening Office. It records a sequence of edits and replays them against other files, which is how you go from one report to forty. And it validates a file's internal structure, which catches corruption before you send something to a client.

Two honest caveats, and I would say both out loud rather than leave them for the comments. The first is a real bug: there is an open issue filed on August 14th 2026, against the current version, where the import command reports success with correct row and column counts and writes nothing at all to the worksheet. That is the worst class of bug, the kind that lies to you, so build files with the create and set commands rather than importing until it is fixed. The second is about trust. The project has 28,601 stars and ships under Apache 2.0, which sounds reassuring, but it comes from a small organisation with no public members whose other main project also carries an outsized star count, and the install is a curl-piped-to-bash command with no checksum step. There is also a completely separate, unrelated project with almost the same name, so it is genuinely easy to install the wrong thing from a search result. None of that means it is bad software — the capabilities check out against the documentation and the release cadence is real. It means I would install it into a project directory I do not mind rebuilding rather than onto a machine holding anything sensitive, and I would say so plainly rather than pretend the question does not arise.

**How you set it up**
Prerequisites: a Mac or Linux shell with curl, or Windows PowerShell. Claude Code already installed. No account, no signup, no API key for OfficeCLI itself.

1. Install it: `curl -fsSL https://raw.githubusercontent.com/iOfficeAI/OfficeCLI/main/install.sh | bash` on macOS or Linux. On Windows PowerShell, run `irm https://raw.githubusercontent.com/iOfficeAI/OfficeCLI/main/install.ps1 | iex` instead.
2. Verify with `officecli --version`. A version string means you are set; `command not found` means the installer's PATH change has not reached your current shell, so open a new terminal.
3. Register the MCP server with Claude Code: `officecli mcp claude`.
4. Confirm it registered: `officecli mcp list` should show Claude Code.
5. Restart Claude Code, or start a new session, so it picks up the server.
6. Smoke-test the binary on its own before trusting the agent with it: `officecli create deck.pptx` then `officecli watch deck.pptx`, which should open a live preview that refreshes as the file changes.
7. From inside Claude Code, ask for a spreadsheet with an actual formula or pivot table. It should call the OfficeCLI tools rather than writing Python or raw XML.
8. This is the step where people get stuck: after several edits in a row, a command may fail with the file being held by a resident process, or another application may keep showing stale contents. Run `officecli save` or `officecli close file` before anything external reads the file.
9. Check the finished file's structure with `officecli validate file`. Clean output means no structural problems; anything else flags a format error before you send the document anywhere.
10. Avoid `officecli import` for now — see the open bug where it reports success and writes nothing.

## B3 · TUTORIAL · Four agents race one bug

**The script**
Here is the job. You have a bug, and you have opinions about which coding agent is best at this kind of thing, and those opinions are mostly vibes because you have never run them against the same problem at the same time. This setup does that. You describe the bug once, hand it to Claude Code, Codex, Cursor and GitHub Copilot simultaneously, and each one works in its own isolated copy of the repository. Twenty minutes later you have four finished diffs solving the same problem, side by side, and you keep the one you like. The tool that orchestrates it is called Emdash, and its own description of itself is exactly one line: run multiple coding agents in parallel, use any provider.

The mechanism underneath is simpler than it sounds and it is the reason this works at all: git worktrees. A worktree lets one repository have several working directories checked out at once, each on its own branch, sharing a single history. So each agent gets a real directory with real files it can edit freely, with no ability to trip over another agent's half-finished changes, and no containers or virtual machines involved. Emdash's own framing is that each task runs in its own git worktree so you can explore multiple fixes at once, review the diffs, and merge what works. That last part is what you are paying for, honestly — a competent engineer could script worktree creation in an afternoon. What you cannot easily build in an afternoon is the surface around it: automatic detection of which agent CLIs you have installed, a single view to compare diffs, open pull requests and inspect continuous-integration checks, and the ticket integrations that pull work in from Linear, Jira, GitHub and half a dozen others.

The setup has one genuine trap in it, and it is worth being honest about. Emdash does not install or log into the agents for you. It detects provider command-line tools that are already on your machine — nine of them are supported, including Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, OpenCode, Amp, Devin, Qwen Code, Droid and GitHub Copilot — which means you install and authenticate each one yourself first. The failure mode is specific and confusing if you have not been warned: Emdash sees the binary on your PATH and cheerfully lists the agent as available, then the task dies immediately with an authentication error from the provider rather than from Emdash. So log into every CLI in a plain terminal and confirm each one runs on its own before you open the app. The documentation says only that some provider CLIs require account authentication or product access, and does not give you the login commands, so that verification step is yours.

The thing worth watching is a grid. Four panes, four agents, one task description, all running at once — and what makes it watchable is that they diverge visibly. One starts editing immediately, one spends two minutes reading files first, one writes a test before touching the fix. Then the diff view, where you compare four solutions to an identical problem, and the merge, where one of them becomes a pull request and the other three worktrees get thrown away. If you want the comparison to actually mean something rather than just look impressive, pick a bug with a real judgement call in it — something where a lazy fix and a correct fix look different — because on a trivial bug all four will produce nearly the same patch and there is nothing to choose between them.

Where else this goes, once it is standing up: you can point one provider at several different tasks at once instead of several providers at one task, which is the same isolation mechanism used for plain fan-out. You can pipe real tickets in from Linear or Jira and have an agent start on them without copy-pasting. You can point the whole thing at a remote machine over SSH rather than running four agents on your laptop's fans. And you can use it purely as a review hub for agent branches, since the diff, pull request and CI views work regardless of who wrote the code.

The honest costs are the reason I would not run this on every task. The obvious one is money: to race four providers you need four subscriptions, and at current pricing Claude Pro is 20 dollars a month, ChatGPT Plus is 20 for Codex, Cursor Pro is 20, and GitHub Copilot Pro is 10 — about 70 dollars a month in stacked subscriptions before a single extra API token, and four agents each burn a full task's worth of tokens to produce three answers you throw away. The less obvious cost is that the tooling is young. There is an open issue from August 10th 2026 where the Claude Code terminal interface renders corrupted inside Emdash, with frames overdrawing each other or the transcript blanking out, which hits precisely the many-agents-in-one-window case this exists for. And there is a second one from August 11th where the isolation mechanism itself buckles under load: around 250 concurrent worktree listings drove the machine's load average past 200. The latest stable release is version 1.1.40 from July 17th 2026, with canary builds since, and the repository has 5,421 stars, so this is a young project rather than a settled one. I think the comparison is worth running deliberately — on a hard bug, when the answer matters, as an experiment you learn something from — and not worth leaving on as your default way of working.

**How you set it up**
Prerequisites: macOS with Homebrew, and Node.js with npm on your PATH. Accounts that must already exist: Anthropic for Claude Code, OpenAI for Codex, Cursor for its agent. Emdash itself needs no account and issues no API key — every provider authenticates itself. Windows and Linux installers exist, but the steps below are the Mac path.

## C1 · EXPLAINER · Your GPU is mostly waiting

**The script**
Today we are talking about why a chatbot answers at the speed it does, because the explanation you have probably heard is not quite right. The popular account is that the chip is working extremely hard — that generating each word takes an enormous amount of arithmetic, and that the answer arrives one word at a time because the machine is thinking as fast as it can. And that account is not stupid. The arithmetic really is enormous, and a data centre full of these chips really does consume the power of a small city. But when a model is generating text for you, one token at a time, the arithmetic units on that chip are mostly idle. The bottleneck is not the calculation. It is fetching the numbers to calculate with.

One way to think about this is a chef in a very strange kitchen. The chef is superhumanly fast — give them the ingredients and they can chop, mix and plate a dish essentially instantly. But the pantry is down a long corridor, and the rule of this kitchen is that you may only carry one ingredient at a time. So the chef walks to the pantry, collects a carrot, walks back, chops it in a fraction of a second, and walks back for the next thing. If you measured where that day went, almost none of it would be cooking. It would be corridor. And the fix that would obviously help is not hiring a faster chef — the chef is already faster than the job requires — it is either widening the corridor or, better, cooking for forty people at once so that each trip to the pantry feeds forty plates instead of one. Hold on to that last idea, because it turns out to be exactly what large-scale AI serving does.

The numbers make this concrete, and they come from the specification sheet of the chip everybody has been fighting over. NVIDIA's H100 in its SXM form delivers about 1,979 teraflops of dense half-precision tensor throughput — a teraflop being a trillion arithmetic operations a second — against 3.35 terabytes per second of memory bandwidth. Divide one by the other and you get the ratio that matters: this chip can perform roughly 590 arithmetic operations for every single byte it manages to pull out of memory. That ratio has a name, machine balance, and it comes from the roofline model that Samuel Williams, Andrew Waterman and David Patterson published in 2009, which says a program's speed is capped either by peak arithmetic or by peak memory bandwidth, whichever limit it hits first. Now ask what generating one token actually requires. The model reads each of its weights out of memory, multiplies it once, adds it once, and discards it. That is on the order of one or two operations per byte fetched. Against a chip built to do 590, the arithmetic units are running at a fraction of one percent of what they could do.

You can turn that into a speed limit with one division. Take a model with 70 billion parameters stored at two bytes each: that is 140 gigabytes of weights that must be read out of memory to produce one single token. Divide 140 gigabytes by 3.35 terabytes per second, and you get roughly 24 tokens per second as an absolute ceiling for one user's request on one chip — a limit set entirely by how fast bytes can move, in which the chip's headline arithmetic figure plays no part whatsoever. Horace He put the consequence bluntly in a well-known write-up on deep learning performance: if you are spending all of your time doing memory transfers, then increasing the arithmetic throughput of your GPU will not help. And this is why batching is the central trick of AI serving. Run many users' requests through the same loaded weights at once, and one expensive trip to the pantry feeds all of them — which is why your requests get grouped rather than each running the instant it arrives.

Here is where the tidy version stops holding, and it is worth saying because it is the part that gets overstated. This is a description of one phase, not of the whole system. When the model first reads your prompt — the phase called prefill — it processes all those tokens together, so each weight it loads gets used many times over, and that phase genuinely is compute-bound. Same chip, same model, opposite bottleneck, seconds apart. And the picture shifts again with newer architectures: mixture-of-experts models, where only a fraction of the network activates per token, stay memory-bound out to much larger batch sizes than a dense model would, because the batch gets split across experts. Techniques like speculative decoding exist precisely to trade the spare arithmetic for saved bandwidth, using a small cheap model to guess several tokens ahead so a big model can check them all in one pass. And a chip that is waiting is not a chip that is resting: the H100 draws up to 700 watts whether its arithmetic units are busy or stalled, because memory controllers, caches and static power do not care why.

I think the broader point is more interesting than the trivia about chips, and it is this: the constraint on AI right now is less about thinking than about moving. That is why memory has become the thing the industry actually fights over — Samsung and SK hynix raised high-bandwidth memory prices by close to 20 percent for 2026 — and why so much of the engineering effort in serving these models goes into memory management rather than mathematics. The vLLM project, which came out of a 2023 paper on this exact problem, found existing systems were wasting an estimated 60 to 80 percent of their memory to fragmentation, and got two to four times the throughput by fixing it. None of that is about making the chip calculate faster. It is all about the corridor.

## C2 · EXPLAINER · The middle of the context window

**The script**
Today we are talking about long context windows, because the explanation you have probably heard is not quite right. The story goes that a model with a million-token context window can read a million tokens — that if you paste in the whole contract or the whole codebase, the model has all of it available and will use it. And there is a real basis for that story. The information genuinely is in the window, the model genuinely can attend to any position in it, and the attention mechanism that transformers are built on is mathematically capable of reaching any token from any other token. So the assumption that it reads the lot is not naive. It is just not what the measurements show. Where a fact sits inside that window changes how likely the model is to use it, and the shape of that effect is stranger than a simple falling-off.

One way to think about it is a meeting you sat through. Somebody talked for an hour, and you were present and paying attention the whole time. Days later, what do you actually have? You have the opening — the thing they led with, when you were fresh and it was framed as important. And you have the ending — the last few minutes, the decision, the thing that was still in your head when you stood up. The middle forty minutes are not gone exactly; you were there, you would recognise a claim from it if somebody read it back. But if I ask you to retrieve a specific number from minute thirty-two, you will probably miss it. Nobody would say you did not attend the meeting. It is that presence and retrieval are different things, and the position of something inside a long stretch changes which one you get.

The measurement comes from a 2023 study called *Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts*, by Nelson Liu, Kevin Lin, John Hewitt and colleagues, published in the journal Transactions of the Association for Computational Linguistics. The setup is clean: give the model a set of documents where exactly one contains the answer to a question and the rest are plausible distractors, then slide the answer-bearing document from the first position to the last and watch what happens. With twenty documents, GPT-3.5-Turbo answered correctly 75.8 percent of the time when the answer was in the first position, fell to 53.8 percent when it sat in the middle, and recovered to 63.2 percent at the end. That is a 22-point swing produced by nothing but moving the same paragraph around. And the paper's most uncomfortable finding is what that middle number should be compared against: the same model answering from memory with no documents at all scored 56.1 percent. Burying the correct answer in the middle of the retrieved context made it perform worse than giving it nothing.

Two details stop this being a story about one model having a quirk. The first is that the same U-shaped curve showed up on a purely synthetic task — retrieving a value from a list of random key-value pairs, with no language understanding involved at all — which means it is about position, not about reading comprehension. The second is the comparison the authors ran between models and their own extended-context siblings, and the finding is worth quoting: when the input fits in the context window of both a model and its extended-context counterpart, their performance as a function of the position of relevant information is nearly superimposed. A bigger window did not fix the problem. It gave the model more room to be lost in. I should be careful here, though, because the paper demonstrates the effect without proving its cause — the authors note the resemblance to the serial-position effect in human memory, and call it perhaps surprising given that attention is technically capable of retrieving any token, but that is a comparison and an admission, not a mechanism.

The obvious objection is that this is a 2023 result about 2023 models, and models have moved on enormously. That objection is partly right and it matters, so here is what the follow-up work found. A 2025 benchmark called NoLiMa tested twelve models advertised with context windows of 128,000 tokens or more, and its design removes the crutch that makes needle-in-a-haystack tests easy: it strips the literal word overlap between the question and the answer, so the model has to make an actual associative link rather than pattern-match a string. Under those conditions GPT-4o fell from an almost perfect 99.3 percent at short context to 69.7 percent at 32,000 tokens, and eleven of the twelve models tested dropped below half of their own short-context performance at that length. So the specific 2023 numbers are dated, and the effect is not. It also varies a lot by model — in the original study Claude-1.3 was strikingly flat across positions, moving only about three points from first to last, so this was never a universal law so much as a property that differs between systems.

I think the broader point is more useful than the finding itself, which is that a context window is a capacity, not a promise. The number in the specification tells you what the system will accept without erroring, and it tells you nothing about the retrieval accuracy you will get at that length — those are different claims, and only one of them is being marketed. The practical consequence is small and immediate: put the thing that matters at the start or the end of a long prompt rather than the middle, order retrieved documents so the best one is not buried, and if a long-context setup matters to your work, test it at the length you actually use rather than trusting the specification. And the honest limit is that this varies enough between models that I would not treat any single number here as a constant — including these ones.

## C3 · EXPLAINER · When nearest stops meaning anything

**The script**
Today we are talking about how AI search actually finds things, because there is a famous result about it that is usually explained slightly wrongly, and the correct version is more interesting than the scary version. Nearly every AI system that looks things up — every retrieval setup, every vector database, every "chat with your documents" product — works by turning text into a long list of numbers called an embedding, and then finding the stored embedding closest to your query. Typically that list is 1,536 numbers long, or 3,072 for the larger models. And there is a well-known theorem saying that in spaces with that many dimensions, the concept of "closest" falls apart: everything ends up roughly equidistant from everything else, so the nearest neighbour is barely nearer than the farthest one. You will see people conclude from this that vector search is fundamentally broken. That conclusion is wrong, and the reason it is wrong is the good part.

One way to think about the underlying effect is a party. Imagine one person in a room and you ask who is nearest to them — easy question, obvious answer. Now imagine everyone in a city, positioned not by geography but by every dimension you can think of at once: age, job, music taste, hometown, politics, and a thousand more. As you add dimensions, everyone differs from everyone else in some way, and the total amount of difference between any two people starts to look about the same. The most similar person to you is not much more similar than a stranger, because both of them differ from you along nine hundred of the thousand axes. That is distance concentration, and it is real. But notice what the party thought experiment quietly assumed: that the thousand traits are unrelated to each other, scattered independently. Real people do not work like that. Neither do real embeddings, and that is the whole ballgame.

The theorem comes from a 1999 paper by Kevin Beyer, Jonathan Goldstein, Raghu Ramakrishnan and Uri Shaft, titled "When Is Nearest Neighbor Meaningful?" Their result is that under a broad set of conditions, as dimensionality increases, the distance to the nearest data point approaches the distance to the farthest one. And in their own synthetic experiments the collapse arrives fast: with randomly scattered independent data, the ratio between the farthest and nearest distances was on the order of ten million in one dimension, had shrunk by six orders of magnitude by ten dimensions, and by twenty dimensions the farthest point was only about four times the distance of the closest. They concluded that nearest-neighbour search can become unstable with as few as ten to twenty dimensions. If that applied straightforwardly to a 1,536-dimensional embedding, retrieval would be hopeless.

It does not apply straightforwardly, and the paper says so itself. The theorem carries a precondition: the spread of the distances has to shrink relative to their average as dimensions increase. Where that does not happen, the theorem does not apply. And in 2009, Robert Durrant and Ata Kabán proved the matching converse, showing that if the relative variance of the distances does not tend to zero, then neither does their relative separation. In other words, concentration is not a consequence of high dimension as such; it is a consequence of a particular kind of structurelessness. Their counter-example class is data generated from a smaller set of underlying latent factors, which is close to a description of what an embedding space is built to be. Here is the part I found genuinely surprising: they also tested and rejected the tidy explanation most people reach for, which is that real data is saved by having a low intrinsic dimension. They give worked examples where the number of underlying factors grows without limit and concentration still does not occur. What governs it is the correlation structure of the data, not a count of hidden dimensions. Their own experiments show both outcomes side by side — a gene-expression dataset where every point had half the data within a factor of 1.669 of its nearest neighbour, and four text-retrieval datasets of comparable dimensionality where, in their words, the distances are well spread out, so the nearest neighbour is indeed meaningful.

There is a separate effect worth knowing, because it explains something you may have noticed. Real embedding spaces are anisotropic — they occupy a narrow cone rather than spreading evenly in all directions. Kawin Ethayarajh measured this in 2019 and found that in GPT-2's middle layers, two randomly chosen words had an average cosine similarity of around 0.6, and by the final layer almost any two words looked nearly identical by that measure. That is why similarity scores from an embedding model cluster in a narrow band instead of spanning zero to one, and why a raw score of 0.8 tells you much less than it sounds like it should. The distinctions are still in there, but they are packed into a far smaller region than the raw dimension count suggests.

I think the broader point is more interesting than the theorem, and it is about what a proof actually promises. The 1999 result is correct — about randomly scattered independent data, which is exactly what a meaningful embedding is engineered not to be. The whole job of an embedding model is to put related things near each other, and that clustering is precisely the condition under which the theorem's precondition fails. Beyer and his co-authors even anticipated the resolution, noting that when data is clustered and a query lands near a cluster, the proper response is to return all the points in that cluster rather than a single nearest point — almost exactly what a retrieval system does when it fetches the top several passages instead of one. So the honest version is not that vector search is broken. It works for a reason, and the reason is a structural property of the data rather than of the algorithm — a property you can lose. If your embeddings ever stop being well-clustered for your domain, the mathematics is waiting.

## D1 · LONGFORM · Renting the chip you cannot sell

**Hook** (0-15s, the cold open before any title card)
> It is illegal to sell an Nvidia Blackwell processor to a Chinese AI company. It is not illegal to rent one to them. That distinction is worth billions of dollars, and it survives because American export law was built to stop objects crossing borders, at a time when nobody imagined the object would stay still and the border would be crossed by a login.

**What this is**
Since 2022 the United States has restricted the sale of its most advanced AI chips to China, and those restrictions have tightened repeatedly since. The intent is not subtle: keep the best hardware out of the hands of Chinese AI labs. But the rules that implement that intent are written in terms of physical movement. The Export Administration Regulations define an export as an actual shipment or transmission out of the United States, and the secondary definition, the deemed export, covers releasing technology or source code to a foreign person inside the United States. Neither one describes what happens when a Chinese company signs a contract with a data centre in Osaka or Kuala Lumpur and runs its training job on chips that never come within a thousand miles of China.

That gap is not theoretical and it is not small. In December 2025 the Financial Times reported that Tencent had secured roughly 1.2 billion dollars in contracts covering most of 15,000 Nvidia Blackwell processors hosted by the Japanese firm Datasection across data centres in Osaka and Sydney, on a three-year lease. The Wall Street Journal traced a Shanghai AI startup to 2,304 Blackwell GPUs hosted by an Indonesian telecoms operator. And Bloomberg's reporting on a Singapore company called Megaspeed described more than 136,000 GPUs and 4.6 billion dollars of Nvidia hardware imported since 2023, with sites that officials believe serve Chinese customers remotely. What makes this worth ten minutes rather than a headline is that almost none of it appears to be illegal, the people saying so include the lawyers and the regulator, and the one attempt to change that passed the House of Representatives 369 votes to 22 and then stopped dead.

**The thesis**
The offshore GPU rental trade is not a smuggling story but a drafting story: export control regulates the movement of things, compute is sold as a service rather than moved as a thing, and Washington has understood the mismatch since at least January 2026 without closing it — because the fix requires the one thing the current administration has been unwilling to give up, which is discretion over who gets licensed.
