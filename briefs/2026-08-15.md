## A2 · NEWS · Six grid operators, one deadline

**Hook** (0-3s)
> On Monday, six companies have to explain why your power bill should pay for a data center.

**Why it's good**
There is a clock on this one and almost nobody outside energy law is watching it. On June 18 FERC issued Section 206 show-cause orders to all six US grid operators at once — PJM, MISO, SPP, CAISO, ISO-NE and NYISO — and did something unusual: it declared their existing tariffs for large loads presumptively unjust. That flips the burden. Normally a regulator has to prove a rule is broken; here the operators have 60 days to prove theirs isn't. Sixty days from June 18 is August 17. The specific question they have to answer is who pays for the substations and transmission when a 20-megawatt-plus load shows up, and the honest answer for the last two years has been "everyone else's bill."

**The beats**
- 0-3s — The deadline and what is being asked.
- 3-15s — Six orders, six dockets, one day. The threshold is 20 MW. A large data center is many multiples of that.
- 15-35s — What "presumptively unjust" means: FERC did not ask them to consider a change, it put the burden of proof on them to defend the status quo.
- 35-60s — Watch what actually gets filed Monday. A defence means your bill keeps absorbing it; a reform filing means the data centers start paying their own interconnection costs.

## C1 · EXPLAINER · A data center is a heater

**The misconception**
That a data center uses electricity to do computing, and cooling is an unfortunate side cost on top. It is closer to the reverse: essentially all the electricity becomes heat, and the computing is something that happens on the way.

**Hook** (0-3s)
> A data center converts almost one hundred percent of its electricity into heat. The thinking is a side effect.

**The analogy**
An electric space heater is 100% efficient by definition — every watt in becomes a watt of heat out. A data center is the same device with better manners. Nothing leaves the building as "computation"; a rounding error leaves as light down a fibre. The rest leaves as warm air, which is why cooling is not overhead sitting beside the power bill — it is the same watt showing up on the bill a second time.

**Where the analogy breaks**
It does not tell you computing is wasteful. The physical floor for erasing a bit is real but is roughly a billion times below what actual chips use, so thermodynamics is not the binding constraint — engineering is. And the heat is not always wasted: some sites now sell it into district heating, which is the one case where the space-heater framing becomes literally the business model.

**The beats**
- 0-3s — The claim, flat.
- 3-15s — Follow one watt in. Show where it can possibly go. There is nowhere else.
- 15-35s — So cooling is not a tax on the power bill, it is the second half of the same watt. That is why siting is really about water and air, not land.
- 35-60s — Pairs to the news: this is what the grid operators are actually arguing about on Monday.

## C2 · EXPLAINER · Why long chats get expensive

**The misconception**
That a model reads your message once, the way you read a message once. It re-reads the entire conversation from the beginning to produce every single word it says back.

**The analogy**
A dinner party where, before anyone speaks, every guest has to shake hands with every other guest. Ten guests is manageable. A hundred guests is not ten times worse, it is a hundred times worse — the handshakes grow with the square of the table. That is attention. Each new token has to consult every token before it, so the cost of a conversation does not grow with its length, it grows with the square of its length.

**Where the analogy breaks**
Real systems cheat, legitimately. The key-value cache means the model does not literally redo every handshake for every new word — earlier work is stored and reused, which pulls the per-token cost down to linear. Prompt caching goes further and is the one optimisation that gets cheaper the longer the context runs. So the honest version is: the quadratic term is real and it is why long context was hard, but the industry has spent three years building machinery specifically to avoid paying it.

**The beats**
- 0-3s — The reframe: it re-reads everything, every time.
- 3-15s — The handshake table. Draw it growing.
- 15-35s — Why you feel it: the long thread that got slow, the API bill that grew faster than the transcript.
- 35-60s — Then the honest correction — caching. And the reason that matters this week is that compute scarcity is now showing up directly in prices.

**Sources**
- DigitalOcean, long-context inference cost in production — https://www.digitalocean.com/community/tutorials/long-context-inference-production-cost
- FastKV, prefill and KV-cache costs — https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.01068

## C3 · EXPLAINER · The digit that catches fraud

**The misconception**
That made-up numbers look random, and that looking random is what makes them convincing. It is the opposite — real-world numbers are lopsided in a very specific way, and fabricated ones are usually too evenly spread.

**The analogy**
Watch a number grow by percentages instead of by addition. To climb from 1,000 to 2,000 it has to gain 100%, so it spends a long time starting with a 1. To get from 9,000 to 10,000 it needs only 11%, so it barely pauses on 9. Anything that grows this way — invoices, populations, river lengths, expenses — piles up on the low digits. First digit 1 shows up about 30.1% of the time. Digit 9 shows up about 4.6%. A fraudster inventing invoices reaches for 800 and 900 because they want the money, and flattens the curve.

**Where the analogy breaks**
It is a red flag, not a verdict, and it fails cleanly in predictable places: numbers with hard floors and ceilings, assigned numbers like ZIP codes, and prices deliberately set at $9.99. It also cannot catch a single theft — it needs a population of numbers to test. Courts in the US do accept it, but as one tool among several, never as proof on its own. The honest ending is that Benford's law tells you where to look, and then a human still has to go look.

**The beats**
- 0-3s — Fake numbers do not look random enough. That is how they get caught.
- 3-15s — The 30.1% / 4.6% split. Show the curve.
- 15-35s — Why it happens: percentage growth spends longer in the low digits. The 1,000-to-2,000 versus 9,000-to-10,000 image.
- 35-60s — Where it fails, and the live example — Jefferies was told this month that invoices behind its financing to an iron ore trader were not genuine, which is exactly the shape of data this test is built for.

**Sources**
- ACFE, why fraud examiners use it — https://www.acfe.com/acfe-insights-blog/blog-detail?s=what-is-benfords-law-and-why-fraud-examiners-use-it
- Significance (Royal Statistical Society), detecting the dubious digits — https://rss.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/j.1740-9713.2007.00234.x
- Benford's law, distribution and limits — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benford's_law
- Bloomberg, the Jefferies fake-invoice story — https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-04/jefferies-gets-new-evidence-of-fake-invoices-at-beleaguered-fund

**Freshness** evergreen, with a live hook · **Saturation** medium — the law is well covered, the "too evenly spread" framing and the honest limits usually are not

## D1 · LONGFORM · The rating nobody was meant to read

**Hook** (0-15s, the cold open before any title card)
> There is a credit rating agency with about twenty analysts that graded more than three thousand private credit investments in a single year. US insurers are holding roughly forty billion dollars of debt it graded. And you cannot read a single one of those ratings, because they were never published. They were never meant to be.

**The thesis**
Private letter ratings did not inflate because someone was tricking investors — there are no investors reading them. They inflated because the only audience is a regulatory capital formula, and the company buying the rating is the same company the rating lets hold less capital.

**Why it holds ten minutes**
It has a real turn in the middle. The first three minutes look exactly like a 2008 rerun — a small agency, fees for grades, a whistleblower suit — and the viewer arrives already knowing this story. Then the second act takes it away from them: these ratings are unpublished, so the usual fraud framing does not fit. The back half brings in material almost no coverage uses: the NAIC's own 2024 study, which sampled 109 private letter ratings and found 106 of them rated higher than the NAIC's own analysts assessed — 17 of them investment-grade on paper while the NAIC read them as junk, some inflated by six notches. Then the arithmetic that makes it worth doing: moving a credit six notches, from single-B to triple-B, cuts a life insurer's pre-tax risk-based capital charge by roughly 84%. Then the detail that turns it from a finance story into something worse: the NAIC pulled that report off its own website in May 2025 to "clarify" its findings, after industry pushback, and has never republished it. The third act follows the money offshore, and the last two minutes are genuinely live — the NAIC adopted Actuarial Guideline 55 this month.

**The chapters**
- 0:00-0:45 — Twenty analysts, three thousand ratings, forty billion dollars, zero published pages. Sit on the arithmetic.
- 0:45-2:00 — What a private letter rating is and why it exists at all: insurers must hold capital against what they own, and how much depends on a letter grade. No grade, no cheap capital.
- 2:00-4:00 — Act one, the story you think this is. Egan-Jones: two former executives suing, alleging they were fired after raising conflicts with regulators; an SEC probe into its ratings practices; Bermuda quietly removing it from the recognised-ratings list in January. The firm denies all of it.

Roughly two trillion dollars of US life liabilities ceded to offshore and captive reinsurers; Bermuda's long-term reinsurers holding $1.52 trillion, over 80% of it originating in the US. The privately graded paper backs retirement annuities, and it is increasingly not in the country that sold them.
- 8:30-10:00 — The turn to now: Actuarial Guideline 55, adopted this month, extends asset adequacy testing to reinsured blocks for the first time; Treasury Secretary Bessent has met state regulators about it. Then the honest uncertainty — none of this has broken. Defaults are low. The open question is not whether the number was a lie, it is whether the number was ever measuring anything.
